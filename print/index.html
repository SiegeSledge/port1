Tailor of Traffic

A satirical fashion-commerce game: the weirder the garment, the better it sells, until the buyer's photo and the returns expose the illusion.

  • Systems design
  • Economy design
  • Information design
  • Three.js
  • Unity
  • Bilingual

Overview

Tailor of Traffic is a bilingual browser game about a fashion designer who learns to make clothes for an attention economy. The player assembles a garment, stages and retouches the sale photo, sets a price and production quantity, and then sees what customers actually received. A flood of orders can still lose money once the returns arrive.

The project grew from one observation about online fashion retail: a bizarre garment can draw attention, product photography can make it desirable, and the customer’s photo can undo the illusion in seconds. A Unity port of the full game was built afterwards.

The premise

A designer is repeatedly rejected by the market. Two scripted failures establish the stakes, and then the designer awakens a “system” that shows how popular each design element is. From then on the designer stitches trending keywords together into ever more absurd hits. The tone is absurd, cheerful and satirical, and the central image is a designer adding a sixth sleeve to a shirt because it might sell, while thinking this shirt is stupid.

Reference points from the concept notes:

  • Game Dev Tycoon: creative decisions tied to business results.
  • Reigns: simple decisions with consequences that emerge later.
  • Papers, Please: a theme expressed through the work the player repeats.
  • Good Pizza, Great Pizza: a familiar job made funny by its customers.

The defining application of these ideas is the side-by-side reveal of the same garment in a polished seller photo and an unflattering buyer photo.

Core loop: three stages, three seasons

1. Design

Each season starts at the cutting table. The player picks one of two base patterns (a shirt for 80 yuan or a dress for 100 yuan), chooses from five fabrics, and attaches up to 24 parts across the neck, arms, chest, hem and extras. Parts can be duplicated, scaled and moved from slot to slot. An intern tries the garment on in a preview corner and reacts (“Please don’t tell anyone I work here”). Market heat and costs are visible; the garment’s underlying scores stay hidden while editing.

2. Photo shoot

The player picks a model (the intern for a flat 200 yuan, or a supermodel at 15 yuan per second while the clock runs), one of three sets (white studio, worksite or beach) and up to two props. Dragging limb handles poses the model, and three sliders retouch the frame: film mood, fabric polish and golden ratio.

Pulling the golden-ratio slider stretches the model’s proportions in screen space, which also warps the background. On the beach set a heavy edit bends the horizon and the shoreline. Horizons, doorframes and railings become evidence that players can see for themselves.

3. Shop and launch

After the shoot the player sets a retail price and a production run, then decides what to do with unsold stock from earlier seasons. The launch reveals demand, returns, reviews and the balance sheet, and places the polished cover next to the buyer’s photo.

In one Season 1 run, selling 38 units brought in 2,475 yuan, but 13 units came back. After 1,950 yuan of production, 104 yuan of return shipping, the 200 yuan model fee and the 195 yuan sample cost, net profit was 221 yuan.

Between seasons

  • Rent is due every season and trends shift from one season to the next, so a hit in Season 1 does not carry over.
  • The first, panned garment hangs in the workshop, and its condition reflects the designer’s choices across the three seasons; it returns in the ending.
  • The design notes also call for market heat that saturates (if everyone makes short sleeves, short sleeves stop being worth anything), lagging system data and competitors who copy hits, so that players cannot simply optimize against a visible number.

Design principle: information visibility

The design notes give every metric, threshold and event a visibility tier, because what the designer knows is not what the player needs to see.

Tier Meaning Examples
Visible The interface shows the number Style heat, cost, budget
Implied No number; shown through imagery or character reactions The intern’s expression, a bent doorframe, a dusty garment on the wall
Revealed afterwards Unknown until launch or settlement Sales, returns, reviews, which hidden keyword fired
Hidden Never shown; players discover it by experimenting Hidden-keyword conditions, the sweet spot of absurdity

Consequences of this rule:

  • The system shows traffic, not consequences. That is itself the satire.
  • Internal metric names are never the words players see; the interface uses industry jargon instead.
  • No live scores appear while the player designs, poses or retouches. Feedback arrives after the action, at launch or settlement.
  • Jokes are never explained. The seller photo and buyer photo sit side by side and the gap speaks for itself.

Playtest feedback and what changed

Player feedback Response
Players could not remember what the system said during the awakening and could not find it after leaving the board A cross-stage “system” entry that can be opened from the trend board, design, shoot and shop
Numbers and arrows were hard to understand Plain-language explanations of the public heat and direction indicators, inside the system panel
Too many variables at the start Gradual release of indicators across seasons (proposed)
Players wanted to spend earnings on more design options Purchasable unlocks (proposed), with the unlock price and the per-season sample cost stated clearly
Staged props sometimes moved by themselves Traced the cat’s timed movement; other cases still to reproduce

The resulting system panel explains the eight public heat and trend indicators and recalls current and past headlines. Its messages come from existing season data, so it adds no save fields. Opening it keeps the current screen and pauses the model’s billing timer; closing it restores focus.

Technology

Browser version. TypeScript, Three.js and Vite. The scene renders at 480 x 270 with a depth texture; a full-screen pass adds depth outlines and 64-colour OKLab palette matching before nearest-neighbour enlargement. Models are built from boxes, with attachment slots for garment parts, two-bone inverse kinematics for posing, and simplified proxy boxes for picking and occlusion. The proportion edit is a screen-space warp that distorts the background with the model, and the same transformation is implemented in TypeScript for interaction. The simulation layer (garment attributes, demand, returns, season transitions) is written as pure functions with injectable random sources, separate from the renderer. Player-facing text lives in Chinese and English JSON files.

Unity port. Built with Unity 6000.6.9f1, URP 17.6 and the Input System. It keeps the C# simulation layer, voxel export and pixel shader. The full flow (language select, two opening failures, awakening, three seasons, ending and restart) was run in the macOS player in both languages at 1280 x 720, 844 x 390 and 568 x 320.

Process

Work proceeded in milestones: an orthographic pixel renderer and workshop; a deterministic simulation with the specified reference strategies; one playable season; extra sets, props, occlusion and hidden events; then the scripted opening, ending and three-season structure. The bilingual version was developed separately and merged into the main tree. Hand-off notes (AGENTS.md and PROGRESS.md) carried the work between Claude Code and Codex.

Limits and next steps

  • Implementation was done with AI coding tools; the design, specification, trade-offs and playtest judgment are mine.
  • Playtesting so far has been internal. Whether first-time players can explain market heat and use it to choose a design is still an open question.
  • Known defect: if the season board saves before photos finish decoding, earlier photos can turn transparent.
  • The Unity port has no Android build yet, and some buttons are small at 568 x 320.
  • Proposed next iterations: gradual release of indicators and purchasable design options.

Michelin Under the Desk

Overview

Michelin Under the Desk is a single-player browser roguelite about sneaking snacks in class and seeing how far you can take it before the teacher turns around.

The idea started from one small decision: the chalk stops scraping against the board, so do you put the snack away, or finish one more bite? It then became progressively harder to explain: what if the thing you have to hide is a hot pot that is still steaming?

How it plays

  • Hold Space to look down at the desk cubby and eat. Release to look up and check the teacher.
  • Press S while looking down to stash the food. Releasing Space only stops chewing; food can stay on the desk until it is stashed.
  • A lit hot pot keeps steaming even after it has been stashed.
  • The period key toggles a debug overlay (hidden by default).

Structure

The game has a normal classroom, a library and an exam hall, with three 90-second lessons in each, followed by a cooking finale in the director’s office. It includes nine foods, 24 upgrade cards, meta-progression and three endings.

Foods carry different costs. A packet of spicy strips is easy to hide, chips are loud, the smell of luosifen drifts toward nearby seats, and a lit hot pot keeps steaming. Meeting a lesson’s quota unlocks a choice of three upgrade cards between lessons, which shift a run toward eating quietly, swallowing faster, or drawing suspicion elsewhere.

Between runs, an overly serious in-game system treats sneaking snacks like a discipline you can master. Points earned in class unlock story beats in the player’s bedroom. By the time you reach the director’s office, the aroma that used to give you away is what makes the director come to the table.

Design decisions

Make “watching the teacher” a decision

In the first internal playtest I caught myself watching the status light. It told me what the teacher was doing, and a side panel showed sight, noise, scent and seat positions. I moved both behind a debug overlay and replaced them with audio cues: the sound of chalk stopping, two layers of footsteps (heel strike and scuff), and page turns in the library and exam hall. Now the player decides when to stop chewing based on what they hear.

Rebuild the look-down camera

The first low-angle prototype cut from the classroom to a wooden cubby with a snack packet that seemed to float: the cubby filled the frame and there were no hands or sleeves. The sequence was rebuilt around a single seated first-person view. The camera dips in about 0.28 seconds along the same path it returns on, and the same sleeves, fingers and knees connect the motion to the player. The packet slides out of the cubby before turning upright and follows the same path back when S is pressed. Its size stays consistent, and bowls and pots stay level. The pass also fixed two geometry bugs: a size conversion that scaled food twice, and a flat packet that could intersect the back of the cubby.

Audio

The music is lowered while the player looks down so it does not mask chewing or footsteps. The library’s page turns now sound distinct from the player’s snack packaging, and the chalk stroke ends with a sharper tap.

An input edge case

Holding Space and tapping S used to be cancelled by the next frame’s eating input. Now S starts the stash and continued Space keeps the head down until the stash finishes; a fresh press of Space is required to take food out again.

Market scan

Classroom stealth already had references: Worst Class Ever (the developers reported more than 100,000 copies on the first day), Classroom Snacks on itch.io, and Don’t Get Caught. The question I focused on was how the food in your hands should change your next move.

Technology

TypeScript, Vite, Three.js (WebGL2) and Web Audio. The classroom simulation runs on a fixed 60 Hz step so that timing can be tested separately from rendering. All visible models are procedural voxels, with slots reserved for Tripo GLB models.

Demo

The 33-second gameplay recording has stereo in-game audio. It uses a fixed seed and schedules one turn and one patrol so both cues appear in a single continuous take. The footage comes from the game renderer with control captions added and is unedited.

Limits and next steps

  • The browser version was built with AI coding tools from a build brief I wrote; the design decisions above are mine.
  • There has been no outside-player observation and no real listening test yet. The open question is whether a new player hears the chalk stop, puts the snack away, and can explain what gave them away when caught.
  • Known issue: on a phone in portrait mode the “book” button overlaps the food card.

Overview

During a summer UX design internship at NetEase Interactive Entertainment, I worked on two things for Identity V: an AI post-match review assistant for new players, designed from scratch, and a backpack quick-swap interaction for Mojin mode.

Project 1: AI post-match review assistant

The problem

New players can perform the basic actions but struggle to understand the decisions behind a match. Raw replays and text summaries do not tell them where they went wrong or what to practice.

The design

  • Key match events, game-mechanic knowledge and personalized advice are organized into a closed loop: locate the problem, explain why, suggest training.
  • I delivered the core flow, an interaction prototype, a working demo and a multi-agent architecture.
  • In the demo, the AI reads structured match events through Function Calling on a preset event viewer, so its analysis is tied to specific behavior.
  • For talent recommendation and explanation, RAG retrieves the wiki, game terminology and skill counter / combo information, so suggestions combine real match evidence with domain knowledge.

Natural language versus GUI

I explored how language and graphical interface should divide the work in an AI-coach scenario. Natural language handles explanation, follow-up questions and personalized advice. Timestamps, key events and structured information help the player locate the problem, which avoids squeezing a complex match into a text answer that cannot be verified.

Project 2: Backpack quick swap in Mojin mode

The problem

In high-pressure combat the player needs to decide quickly but cannot afford a mis-tap.

The design

  • Item recommendation, preview and confirm, one-tap swap and error-protection mechanisms.
  • Recommendation rules, system states and data requirements defined for engineering.
  • The design moved into test-server validation.

How I worked

  • Held prototype reviews and version validation with design, engineering and art, and followed experience issues through to closure.
  • Wrote interaction flows, recommendation logic and data-interface requirements.
  • Combined AI agents, Figma and code-generation tools for requirements analysis, prototyping and validation, which sped up iteration and cross-team collaboration.
  • Independently wrote the patent-disclosure materials for the UI interaction.

Overview

TransitEase is an app for disabled riders, wheelchair users and people with heavy luggage in the New York subway. It brings real-time conditions, accessible navigation and community feedback into one decision flow. It was my independent project, supervised by Prof. Ji Woo, and the same topic is the subject of my NYU Shanghai capstone paper.

The problem

New York was chosen as a representative test field for two reasons: the MTA’s accessibility problems are widely discussed, and plenty of data and prior research on NYC transportation is available. Despite accessible facilities such as elevators, disabled riders face challenges from both sides:

  • Service providers such as the MTA lack enough accessible infrastructure and cannot gather real-time data on whether accessible facilities work.
  • Users get inaccurate trip planning and route suggestions because map software relies on outdated information about accessible facilities and destinations.

After one failed iteration, the final idea relies on crowdsourcing and universal design.

Research

  • Desktop research and user personas.
  • Interviews with 30+ disabled riders at major subway stations, identifying breakpoints in route planning, facility status and ad-hoc help.

Design

  • Owned the information architecture, key user flows and high-fidelity prototypes: Axure RP for user flows, Figma for wireframes and UI.
  • Built a working prototype with VS Code for the front end, MySQL for the back-end database and the Google Maps API for GPS and map functions, unifying navigation, facility information and community feedback.
  • Added a user community with an embedded map, and tuned information priority and task flow from real usage feedback.
  • Introduced token rewards to support sustained use, and explored revenue through collaboration with charity organizations.

Outcome

  • 200+ loyal users accumulated, which validated the demand for accessible navigation and community information sharing.
  • Capstone paper: Community-based solution to disabled transportation: A community-based AI-empowered solution of routine planning for disabled people (NYU Shanghai, Class of 2025). Keywords: crowd-sourcing, accessible design, universal design, route planning, real-time accessible data.

Immersive Trap

An exhibition-style piece on how AI-driven personalization builds information cocoons and echo chambers.

Role
Project lead
Timeframe
2025
Organization
Personal project
  • Concept design
  • Exhibition
  • AI and media
  • Echo chambers

Overview

Immersive Trap is a spatial exhibition concept that translates algorithmic echo chambers into a physical installation, examining how recommendation systems narrow what people see while feeling effortless and personalized.

Research & concept breakdown

Origins of the problem

  • Psychological foundations: confirmation bias and a preference for familiar viewpoints lead people toward information that reinforces existing beliefs.
  • Algorithmic amplification: recommendation systems popularized in the early 2000s optimize for engagement, unintentionally accelerating information-cocoon effects.
  • Media fragmentation & attention monetization: selective exposure predates the internet, and targeted advertising rewards watch time over viewpoint diversity.

Role of AI

Every click, pause, and view feeds collaborative-filtering and content-based recommendation models, steadily tightening feedback loops and escalating polarization.

Exhibition execution

  • Mapped the formation stages of an echo chamber, from initial feedback loop to long-term narrowing, grounded in YouTube and TikTok recommendation patterns.
  • Translated the research into spatial metaphors, physical model building, and an exhibition walkthrough.

  • Bryant Park is reported at roughly 60% local usage

Overview

Pause NYC tackles the rush-and-check-off pace of New York tourism. By surfacing stories behind buildings and landmarks and turning walking routes into a waypoint “unlock” game, it invites both visitors and locals to explore the city at a slower pace and share their footprints.

Research

  • Local landmark density: more than 300 distinct neighborhoods across five boroughs and over 37,000 landmarked buildings and sites, according to the NYC Landmarks Preservation Commission.
  • Visitor mix: in a typical pre-pandemic year (2018–2019), about 80% of visitors came for leisure and 20% for business; NYC & Company projects total visits approaching or surpassing the 66.6 million pre-pandemic peak as early as 2024–2025.
  • Concentrated hotspots: Empire State Building, Chrysler Building, One World Trade Center, Rockefeller Center, the Statue of Liberty, and Central Park capture most tourist traffic.
  • Locals rarely explore: in a survey of over 1,200 NYC residents, 40% had not visited any of the top-five tourist attractions in the preceding two years.
  • Local benchmark: Bryant Park sees roughly 60% local usage, showing how free programming and open space sustain repeat visits.

Design focus
